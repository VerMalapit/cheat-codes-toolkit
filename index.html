TUTORIAL

Prompts that work

A good prompt has five parts. The context is the part only you know, so never skip it.

Role

Who the AI should act as

You are a Grade 8 science teacher in a public high school in Mindanao.

Context

Your learners and your situation

My class has 40 learners with mixed reading levels and limited internet.

Task

What you want

Create a 50-minute lesson on the water cycle.

Format

How you want it laid out

Use a table: time, activity, teacher’s role, learners’ role.

Constraints

The limits it must respect

Use only materials found in a typical classroom. Include one quick check for understanding.

Ready to copy. Replace the red [brackets] with your own details.

Lesson plan

You are a [grade/subject] teacher in [type of school, place]. My class has [number] learners who [describe needs]. Create a [length]-minute lesson on [topic] that meets this learning outcome: [outcome]. Use a table with time, activity, teacher’s role and learners’ role. Use only [available materials].

Quiz

Create a [number]-item quiz on [topic] for [grade]: [number] multiple choice, [number] true or false and [number] short answer. Include an answer key with a one-line reason for each answer.

Rubric

Create an analytic rubric with [number] criteria and 4 performance levels for this task: [describe task]. Make every descriptor observable, not just “good” or “excellent”.

Differentiation

Rewrite this passage at three levels: support, on-level and extension. Keep the key ideas the same. [Paste passage]

Explainer video script

Write a [2]-minute explainer video script on [one concept] for [grade] learners in [place]. Use simple language and one local example. Split it into scenes, and for each scene give the narration and a short description of what to show on screen.

Then build it in Canva, Clipchamp or Google Vids, check every fact and label, and turn on auto-captions.

The first answer is a draft. Reply with what to change, for example “Make the opening activity more hands-on and local.”

Show it an example. Paste a quiz or worksheet you like and add “Make it like this.” AI copies a good example better than it follows a long description.

One task, one chat. Start a new chat for each task, so old instructions don’t get mixed in.

POWER-UPS

The cheat codes

The only cheat codes allowed in class: seven ways to get more out of any AI assistant, plus the one that matters most.

CHEAT CODE #01

Interview me first

End any prompt with:

Before you write anything, ask me 5 questions about my class.

CHEAT CODE #02

Set your context once

Make one Project per subject in Claude or ChatGPT. Upload your syllabus and a short class profile once, and every chat in that project starts from them. Both are free; ChatGPT’s free plan allows 5 files per project. Never upload learners’ personal information.

CHEAT CODE #03

Make it local

Add one line:

Rewrite every example using [your place]: [local livelihoods, seasons, places].

CHEAT CODE #04

Wrong on purpose

What 3 wrong answers do [grade] learners give most often for [topic], and why?

Use them as the choices in your quiz. When a learner picks one, you know what to reteach.

CHEAT CODE #05

Build it in 60 seconds

Make a 5-question quiz game on [topic] for [grade], as one web page. Show one question at a time with four big buttons, give a point for each right answer, and celebrate at the end.

Play it yourself first, as a learner would, then tell the AI what to fix in plain words.

CHEAT CODE #06

Try it in AI first

Before you give an assignment, try it in AI yourself. If AI can do it in seconds, so can your learners. Then change the task.

CHEAT CODE #07

Practise on a confused learner

You’re a [grade] learner who believes [misconception]. Only change your mind if my explanation convinces you.

Then practise explaining. Useful for pre-service teachers before practice teaching.

CHEAT CODE #08

You

AI is the tool. You are the real cheat code. Nothing to copy here: you already have it.

Build your first AI assistant

My teaching assistant (free, with Projects)

In Claude or ChatGPT, make a Project called “My teaching assistant”. Paste this into its instructions:

You are my teaching assistant. I teach [subject] to [grade or level] at [type of school, place]. My classes: [number of learners, their needs, devices available]. Each time I ask for something, draft it for me to review: lesson plans, quizzes, activities, parent letters, announcements. Use local examples. Ask me questions first if anything is unclear. Never assume details about specific learners. Keep everything short and ready to edit.

Then upload your curriculum guide and syllabus. Every chat in that project is now your assistant.

  1. It drafts, you decide. Nothing goes out without you.
  2. No learner details. Describe needs and patterns, not names.
  3. Before connecting AI to school email or files, check your school’s policy.

WORLD 3 · ASSESSMENT

An AI-use level for every assignment

Write one level on every task sheet, so learners know the rules before they start. Choose the level by what you are assessing.

LevelAI useWhat learners may doGood for
0No AIAll work is their ownIn-class writing, oral exams, core skills
1AI for ideasBrainstorm and plan with AI; nothing AI-written is submittedEssays and projects at the planning stage
2AI for feedbackAI comments on their draft; they do the revisingWriting practice, drafts
3AI as co-writerAI may draft parts; they edit and declare what AI didResearch tasks, reports
4AI as collaboratorFull use, with a reflection on how AI shaped the workReal-world problem solving

Copy onto your task sheet

AI-use level for this task: [0–4]. If you use AI, write one or two sentences explaining what you used it for.

Adapted from the AI Assessment Scale (Perkins, Furze, Roe and MacVaugh, 2024).

WORLD 4 · RESPONSIBLE USE

Before you trust GPT: Generate, Pause, Test

Run through this before any AI output reaches your learners. Artificial Intelligence still needs Actual Intelligence.

G

Generate

P

Pause

T

Test

Tip: use a search tool that shows its sources, like Perplexity. For references, search the title in Google Scholar and paste the DOI into doi.org.

Why AI makes mistakes: it predicts likely words from patterns and, unless it searches the web, doesn’t check facts. Fluent is not the same as understanding, and familiar is not the same as true.

WORLD 4 · RESPONSIBLE USE

What never goes into a chatbot

Whatever you type into a public chatbot leaves your school. Under the Data Privacy Act of 2012 (RA 10173), learners’ personal information must be protected, and DepEd Order No. 003, s. 2026 ties AI use to existing data privacy law.

Never paste

  • Learners’ full names with grades or behaviour notes
  • LRNs, ID numbers, addresses, phone numbers
  • Health, family or counselling details
  • Photos or recordings of learners
  • A whole class list for “individual feedback”

Do this instead

  • Use “Learner A” or “a Grade 9 learner”
  • Leave identifying details out entirely
  • Describe the learning need only, if at all
  • Describe patterns: “Half my class confuses evaporation and condensation”
  • Use school-approved accounts, and check each tool’s data settings

When in doubt, leave it out.

WORLDS 1 AND 2 · TOOLS

Tools from the lecture

Each has a free version. They’re grouped by what they’re best for, but most AI tools now overlap, so treat the groups as starting points. Free plans and limits change often, so check before building a whole lesson around one. Start with one tool and learn it well. Most are built around US and UK curricula, so check alignment with DepEd or CHED requirements.

Live checks

Free-account tip: more players, no upgrade. Host one live game for as many players as your plan allows, then assign the same kahoot a few more times. Each assignment gets its own link and its own player limit, so everyone else can play at their own pace.

Before you adopt any tool, ask

PurposeWhat learning does it serve?
AccessDoes it work on a phone and a weak signal?
PrivacyWhat learner data does it collect?
CostIs the free plan enough, and for how long?
